# 01 — What Lesson 8 teaches

## 1. The one sentence the lesson rests on

> **An alif after zabar makes the sound long.**

| Bare | Zabar | Zabar and alif | Says |
|---|---|---|---|
| ب | بَ | **بَا** | ba → **baa** |
| د | دَ | **دَا** | da → **daa** |
| ل | لَ | **لَا** | la → **laa** |

Nothing about the zabar changes. It is the same stroke, in the same place, saying the same vowel — only for
longer. The alif carries **no mark of its own**; its whole job is to stretch the sound before it. That is why the
lesson is named after the pair and not after the alif: the student does not learn a new letter, they learn what
a letter they already know does when it follows zabar.

## 2. What "knowing the long aa" means here

The same frame `docs/lesson-4/01` and `docs/lesson-7/01` set out, with the skills this lesson needs:

| | Skill | Where it is practised |
|---|---|---|
| A | **Short or long?** — بَ against بَا | the drill, every question: the twin is the same letter with zabar and no alif (`03` §5) |
| B | **Hearing and saying** the difference — "ba" against "baa" | **Say it**, and the by-ear question once there are recordings (`07` §1) |
| C | The alif **joins on**, or **stands apart** after د ذ ر ز و | the board's joined example, shown and never drilled (`02` §3) |
| D | **لَا** is laam and alif, not a new letter | one line and one tile on the board (`02` §4) |
| E | Reading a **real word** with a long vowel in it | the walkthrough and the exercise page (`05`) |

**Skill B is the lesson, and this is the honest problem with it.** Lessons 4–7 could be practised by eye: the
difference between بَ and بِ, or بَ and بً, is a picture, and naming the picture teaches it. Here the picture is
easy — an alif is a whole letter, not a stroke — and the thing that matters is a **sound**, which the drill cannot
test until the teacher has recorded it. So the drill in this lesson is the easy half. It is still worth doing: it
fixes the name ("Baa with zabar and alif") and it makes the student *look for* the alif on every letter. But the
part that makes a reader is saying it out loud and hearing it, and `07` §1 asks about it first.

## 3. What it does **not** teach

- **No madd sign.** آ (alif with a wavy line, U+0622) and the maddah above (U+0653) are a longer stretch with its own
  rules — tajweed, and a later pass (`QAIDA-CONTENT.md`, "Scope for this pass"). This is why alif and hamza are not
  in the table (`02` §2).
- **No counting.** "Hold it for two counts" is how a tajweed teacher measures the natural long vowel. It is true,
  but a beginner who has just met the long vowel needs to hear it, not count it. The board says *long*, and the
  teacher can add the count in a text field (`07` §4).
- **No standing fatha.** The same long aa, written as a small upright alif above the letter (U+0670) instead of a
  whole one, is **Lesson 9**. It matters here only as a warning about which words can be shown (`05` §4).
- **No wow or yaa.** The other two long vowels are lessons 11 and 13.

## 4. Why it comes here

`QAIDA-CONTENT.md` item 8, and a printed Qaida agrees: after the three short vowels and their doubled forms, the
long vowels, **alif first**. Of the three it is the kindest to start with: the alif is the one letter the student
has known longest, it never carries a mark here, and zabar is the mark they have known longest too.

It also opens real words. Almost nothing a beginner would recognise can be written with short vowels alone, and a
great deal can be written once the long aa is there — *qaala*, *kaana*, *kitaabun*, *salaamun*. `05` uses that.

## 5. The honest risk in the pedagogy

**The lesson is too easy to see and too hard to hear.** Three things follow, and all three are in this
specification:

1. The by-ear question format is built and waits for recordings, the way it has since Lesson 4. **Here it matters
   more than it has anywhere else**, so `07` §6 asks the teacher to record this lesson's 27 sounds *before* the 87
   tanween ones.
2. **Say it** (step 7) is the practice that works today: the student says "baa", hears themselves, and — once the
   teacher has recorded it — hears the teacher. Nothing new to build; the item's recording group is new (`03` §8).
3. The **board** puts short and long side by side — ب → بَ → بَا — with arrows between them, so the student is
   comparing lengths rather than remembering one (`04` §4).
